# 1. Purpose of this notebook

Notebook 11 is the **decision layer** of the LTE downlink throughput anomaly-detection project.

It starts from the clean pre-anomaly table produced by Notebook 10 and answers five main questions:

1. **Is this throughput observation statistically abnormal?**
2. **Is it poor relative to what should be achievable under its radio/resource conditions?**
3. **Do causal ML models independently support the conclusion?**
4. **How much impact and confidence should the anomaly receive after fusion?**
5. **Which anomalies/incidents are important and diverse enough to hand to RCA?**

The design is deliberately **statistical-first**. ML is used as an expected-throughput validation layer, not as an opaque replacement for interpretable statistical detection.

The notebook also keeps LSTM and Isolation Forest as validation/context models so the project can demonstrate that model selection was empirical rather than arbitrary. They do not contribute to the final compact RCA JSON.

# 3. Loading the clean table and guarding against leakage

The notebook loads the pre-anomaly table generated by Notebook 10.

It expects a stable `source_index`, session identifier, timestamp, and canonical target:

```text
actual_lte_dl_throughput
```

The pipeline is intentionally designed to start from a table that does not already contain previous anomaly outputs.

This matters because expected-throughput columns, residuals, scores, and anomaly flags are all derived from the target. If they accidentally enter ML predictors, the model can learn the answer it is supposed to predict.

---

# 4. Statistical methods are the primary anomaly detectors

The statistical stage contains both **general distribution-based methods** and **context-conditioned methods**.

The general methods are intentionally retained because they provide data-driven insight without depending entirely on a manually selected throughput threshold.

The notebook keeps:

- fixed 10 Mbps anchor;
- global P10;
- session P10;
- IQR lower fence;
- robust MAD/modified-Z;
- temporal/local drop methods;
- P75 expected-underperformance methods;
- RB efficiency.

The important fusion design is that several correlated low-tail methods remain visible individually but are **not treated as fully independent votes**.

---

# 5. Fixed low-throughput detector

The simplest detector is:


$$
T_i < 10\text{ Mbps} 
$$

where $T_i$ is the current actual LTE DL throughput.

A simple normalized severity is approximately:

$$
S_{fixed} = \text{clip}\left(\frac{10-T_i}{10},0,1\right)
$$


Examples:

```text
T = 9 Mbps  → mild fixed-low evidence
T = 5 Mbps  → stronger evidence
T = 1 Mbps  → very strong evidence
```

## Why keep a fixed threshold?

It provides an operationally intuitive anchor. Anyone can understand “below 10 Mbps.”

## Why is it not enough?

Because 10 Mbps may be:

- normal for one difficult radio context;
- extremely abnormal for another cell/session where 80 Mbps is typical.

Therefore the fixed detector is only one part of the statistical layer.

---

# 6. Global 10th-percentile detector

Let the global throughput distribution be \(T\).

Compute:

$$
P10_{global} = Q_{0.10}(T)
$$

Then:

$$
Flag_{P10,global}(i)=1\quad\text{if}\quad T_i \le P10_{global}
$$

This is a fully empirical low-tail detector.

It asks:

> “Is this sample among the lowest-performing observations in the entire analyzed drive-test population?”

This is valuable because it adapts to the dataset rather than assuming a universal throughput threshold.

---

# 7. Session P10 detector

Global distributions can hide session differences. A particular session may have a much higher or lower normal throughput range.

For session \(s\):

$$
P10_s = Q_{0.10}(T\mid session=s)
$$

The notebook flags a sample when it is below the session’s lower-tail reference and the session P10 itself is not unrealistically high for a “low-throughput” interpretation.

Conceptually:

$$
T_i \le P10_s
$$

with an additional guard that the session’s P10 is at or below approximately 20 Mbps.

## Why the extra guard?

Imagine a very high-capacity session where:

```text
P10 = 65 Mbps
```

A point at 60 Mbps is technically in the lowest 10% of that session but is not necessarily an operational throughput problem worthy of RCA.

The guard prevents “relative low” from automatically meaning “bad.”

---

# 8. IQR lower-fence detector

The Interquartile Range method is a classic robust outlier method.

The notebook applies it in log-throughput space:

$$
X_i = \ln(1 + T_i)
$$

Then compute:

$$
Q_1 = Q_{0.25}(X)
$$

$$
Q_3 = Q_{0.75}(X)
$$

$$
IQR = Q_3-Q_1
$$

The lower fence is:

$$
L = Q_1 - 1.5\cdot IQR
$$

A sample is flagged when:

$$
X_i < L
$$

## Why log-transform throughput?

Throughput is usually positively skewed. A difference between 1 and 10 Mbps is operationally much more significant than the same absolute 9-Mbps difference between 100 and 109 Mbps.

`log1p` compresses the high end and gives the lower tail a more stable robust geometry.

## Why retain IQR even if it overlaps P10/MAD?

Because it provides an independent *statistical formulation* of tail extremeness without requiring a telecom-specific fixed value.

However, the fusion logic does not pretend that IQR, P10, and MAD are three completely independent physical phenomena. Their agreement is grouped as bounded supporting evidence.

---

# 9. Robust MAD / modified-Z detector

Again define:

$$
X_i=\ln(1+T_i)
$$

Compute the median:

$$
m=median(X)
$$

and Median Absolute Deviation:

$$
MAD=median(|X_i-m|)
$$

The modified robust Z-score is:

$$
Z_i^{robust}=0.6745\frac{X_i-m}{MAD}
$$

The notebook uses a lower-tail threshold around:

$$
Z_i^{robust}\le -3
$$

If MAD degenerates to zero, a standard-deviation fallback is used so the calculation remains defined.

## Why MAD?

Mean/std-based Z-scores are strongly influenced by extreme values. MAD is much more robust.

It asks:

> “How far below the robust center is this sample relative to typical robust dispersion?”

This is exactly the kind of data-driven statistical method that does not require knowing in advance that “10 Mbps is the correct threshold.”

---

# 10. Why P10, IQR, and MAD are all retained but not triple-counted

These three methods answer related questions:

```text
P10 → Is the point in the empirical lower tail?
IQR → Is it below a robust quartile-based lower fence?
MAD → Is it extremely far below the robust median?
```

They are useful individually for auditing and threshold validation.

But they often flag overlapping rows because all three describe low-tail behavior.

The final design therefore creates a **low-tail consensus** based on:

```text
global P10
IQR lower fence
robust MAD
```

The number of agreeing methods is counted, and agreement contributes only a **bounded evidence bonus** rather than three independent detector-family votes.

This preserves interpretability without inflating confidence.

The notebook explicitly calculates overlap/Jaccard-style diagnostics so the analyst can see how redundant the methods are on the actual dataset.

---

# 11. Threshold calibration report

The notebook derives the implied throughput cutoffs associated with the statistical methods and compares them with the fixed 10-Mbps anchor.

Examples of quantities reported include:

- global P10 in Mbps;
- IQR lower fence converted back from log space to Mbps;
- robust MAD cutoff converted back to Mbps;
- session-P10 distribution/median;
- flag rates;
- pairwise overlap.

This serves a key project goal:

> Use the dataset-derived statistical methods to **validate whether fixed thresholds and fusion behavior are reasonable**, rather than selecting all thresholds only from intuition.

The notebook does **not** automatically optimize fusion weights on the same anomaly data. That would risk overfitting the detector to this one dataset.

Instead, it exposes diagnostics for expert review.

---

# 12. Rolling/local-drop detector

Absolute low throughput is not the only anomaly type.

A drop from:

```text
100 Mbps → 35 Mbps
```

may be a severe local degradation even though 35 Mbps is not globally “low.”

The notebook uses an approximately 11-sample centered rolling window. With the verified one-second cadence, that is roughly an 11-second local context.

Within each session it computes local statistics such as:

```text
rolling median
rolling P25
```

A rolling/local-drop condition uses concepts like:

- local median must be meaningfully high, approximately ≥10 Mbps;
- current/rolling-median ratio approximately ≤0.50;
- absolute gap from rolling median approximately ≥5 Mbps.

Conceptually:

$$
Ratio_{local}=\frac{T_i}{Median_{local,i}}
$$

$$
Gap_{local}=Median_{local,i}-T_i
$$

Flag when both relative and absolute degradation are large.

## Important causality note

This centered statistical window may include samples after the current point.

Therefore it is an **offline anomaly detector**, not a strict causal forecasting feature.

That is acceptable because final anomaly analysis is offline. It is intentionally excluded from causal ML predictors.

---

# 13. Sudden previous-sample drop

Because samples are exactly one second apart, the previous sample provides a direct one-second temporal comparison.

Let:

$$
T_{i-1}=\text{previous throughput in the same session}
$$

Then:

$$
Ratio_{prev}=\frac{T_i}{T_{i-1}}
$$

$$
Drop_{prev}=T_{i-1}-T_i
$$

The detector requires a meaningful previous throughput, then looks for approximately:

```text
current / previous ≤ 0.50
absolute drop ≥ 5 Mbps
```

This identifies sharp one-second collapses.

It is especially useful for mobility transitions, sudden radio failures, scheduler changes, or session disruptions.

---

# 14. Sustained degradation

A single bad sample and a sustained low-throughput region should not be treated identically.

The notebook derives a sustained-window condition using local rolling statistics such as:

```text
rolling median < 10 Mbps
or
rolling P25 < 5 Mbps
```

combined with direct low-tail evidence.

The important modeling choice is:

> Sustained degradation is mainly a **persistence modifier**, not an entirely separate independent physical detector family.

Why?

If five consecutive points are all low, persistence increases seriousness, but it does not represent a fifth unrelated reason to believe throughput is bad.

---

# 15. Bad-session context

For sessions with enough samples, the notebook computes session-level summaries such as:

- median throughput;
- P25 throughput;
- fraction below 10 Mbps.

A session may be labeled broadly degraded if criteria such as the following hold:

```text
median < 10 Mbps
or P25 < 5 Mbps
or low-throughput ratio ≥ 40%
```

This is contextual information. A bad session alone does not cause every row to become a high-priority anomaly. Rows still require direct local/expected evidence.

---

# 16. Hierarchical radio-conditioned P75 expected throughput

Absolute thresholds cannot answer:

> “Given this radio context, how much throughput should we reasonably have expected?”

The notebook therefore builds a **conditional 75th-percentile expected-throughput reference**.

## 16.1 Why P75?

P75 is not the maximum theoretical throughput. It is a robust high-performing reference:

> “Under similar observed conditions, what throughput level was achieved by the better-performing quarter of samples?”

This makes it useful for identifying underperformance without requiring a perfect physics-based throughput model.

## 16.2 Hierarchical conditioning

The most specific grouping may use a combination such as:

```text
SINR bin
RSRP bin
carrier count
band
EARFCN
```

If a group is too sparse, the notebook backs off through broader levels, eventually reaching a global fallback.

The hierarchy allows the reference to be specific when data supports it and stable when it does not.

## 16.3 Minimum group size

A reference group generally needs at least about 30 observations before its P75 is considered statistically useful.

Sparse groups fall back to broader references.

---

# 17. Bootstrap confidence interval for P75

The notebook bootstraps the P75 estimate for each eligible group.

Conceptually:

1. Sample the group with replacement.
2. Compute P75.
3. Repeat many times.
4. Use lower/upper bootstrap percentiles as a confidence interval.

The strict detector uses the **lower confidence bound** as a conservative benchmark when available.

Why is this good?

Suppose the estimated P75 is 60 Mbps but the group is noisy.

Using a conservative lower bound of 50 Mbps instead of 60 Mbps makes the detector harder to trigger. If actual throughput is still far below 50, the underperformance claim is stronger.

---

# 18. Radio-conditioned P75 anomaly rule

For each row, define:

$$
E_{radio}=\text{radio-conditioned expected P75}
$$

$$
Gap_{radio}=E_{radio}-T_i
$$

$$
Ratio_{radio}=\frac{T_i}{E_{radio}}
$$

The strict logic uses approximate gates such as:

```text
expected throughput ≥ 25 Mbps
actual / expected ≤ 0.35
expected - actual ≥ 15 Mbps
reference sufficiently reliable
```

These conditions combine:

- **absolute opportunity**: expected should be meaningfully high;
- **relative failure**: actual is a small fraction of expected;
- **absolute failure**: the Mbps loss is also large.

Using both gap and ratio avoids distortions.

Example:

```text
Expected 20, actual 5 → ratio bad, but only 15-Mbps opportunity.
Expected 100, actual 35 → 65-Mbps gap, but ratio 0.35.
```

Both dimensions matter.

---

# 19. RB-conditioned P75 expected throughput

A second hierarchy adds resource-demand context, especially the RB usage bucket.

It asks:

> “Under similar radio conditions **and similar scheduler/resource demand**, what P75 throughput is typically achieved?”

This is particularly valuable because low throughput under low demand is less suspicious than low throughput while the scheduler is allocating substantial resources.

The RB-conditioned reference uses similar hierarchy, reliability, group-size, bootstrap, gap, and ratio logic.

When RB evidence is reliable, strict expected-underperformance logic can require medium/high demand.

---

# 20. RB-efficiency anomaly detector

The notebook also computes a resource-efficiency measure:

$$
Efficiency_i=\frac{T_i}{RBusage_i}
$$

A lower-tail reference, approximately the P10 of efficiency under reliable meaningful RB demand, is used to detect cases where a large resource allocation produces very little throughput.

The detector focuses on low throughput together with poor throughput-per-RB efficiency.

This captures an anomaly mechanism that is not identical to simply saying “throughput is low.”

For example:

```text
5 Mbps using very few RBs
```

is different from:

```text
5 Mbps while consuming a large RB allocation.
```

The second case indicates much worse resource efficiency.

---

# 21. Radio-limited context

The notebook defines a conservative poor-radio condition using combinations of:

- very poor RSRP;
- poor SINR;
- high BLER;
- a stricter RSRQ+SINR combination.

RSRQ alone is not treated as sufficient evidence of radio limitation.

A throughput anomaly combined with poor-radio evidence can be classified as radio-limited.

This does **not** mean radio-limited anomalies should be excluded. They are valid anomalies whose RCA may simply be “bad radio conditions.”

That is an important design choice.

---

# 22. CA underperformance context

Carrier aggregation is identified from carrier count and/or SCell evidence.

A strict CA-underperformance case generally requires:

- CA active;
- throughput substantially poor;
- enough expected-throughput opportunity;
- resource-demand support where reliable;
- no obvious severe radio explanation in the stricter CA logic.

This helps identify cases such as:

> “Multiple carriers are active and radio conditions are not clearly poor, yet throughput is much lower than expected.”

CA is treated as a technical case/context, not a completely independent statistical detector family.

---

# 23. Event context

Nearby mobility, RACH, CA-change, or failure events are retained as supporting RCA evidence.

They do not independently create a throughput anomaly.

This avoids the incorrect logic:

```text
handover happened → therefore throughput anomaly
```

Instead:

```text
throughput anomaly detected
+ nearby handover failure
→ stronger RCA clue
```

Past-only event features may also support causal ML forecasting, while symmetric ±windows are only offline context.

---

# 24. Four independent statistical detector families

To prevent correlated flags from inflating confidence, the notebook maps many raw statistical methods into four higher-level independent families.

## Family 1 — LOW LEVEL

Supported by methods such as:

- fixed low throughput;
- global P10;
- session P10;
- IQR;
- robust MAD.

These all describe the absolute/distributional low end of throughput.

## Family 2 — TEMPORAL CHANGE

Supported by:

- rolling/local drop;
- sudden previous-sample drop.

These describe degradation relative to nearby time context.

## Family 3 — EXPECTED UNDERPERFORMANCE

Supported by:

- radio-conditioned P75;
- RB-conditioned P75.

These compare actual throughput with context-specific opportunity.

## Family 4 — RB EFFICIENCY

Supported by throughput-per-RB inefficiency under meaningful demand.

This family abstraction is central to defensible fusion.

Ten raw flags do not automatically mean ten independent reasons.

---

# 25. Statistical anomaly score

The statistical score is not a simple sum of flags.

It combines:

1. **base floors for important anomaly types**;
2. **continuous magnitude evidence**;
3. **small bounded bonuses for independent-family agreement and context**;
4. **uncertainty handling for low RB demand**;
5. **soft compression of very high non-catastrophic scores**;
6. **strict catastrophic conditions for 100**.

---

# 26. Statistical base score

Different anomaly mechanisms receive different minimum seriousness floors.

Conceptually, stronger evidence types receive higher base floors, for example:

```text
severe reliable P75 underperformance → high floor
reliable expected underperformance  → substantial floor
RB-efficiency failure               → substantial floor
temporal collapse                   → substantial floor
sustained/radio-limited context     → moderate floor
simple low-tail evidence            → lower floor
```

This prevents a severe expected-throughput collapse from receiving the same starting severity as a mild percentile flag.

---

# 27. Continuous magnitude score

The notebook builds a magnitude score from interpretable dimensions.

A simplified representation is:

$$
Magnitude=100\left(
0.35S_{ratio}
+0.25S_{gap}
+0.25S_{low}
+0.15S_{drop}
\right)
$$

where:

- $S_{ratio}$: how small actual/expected is;
- $S_{gap}$: how large the expected-minus-actual Mbps gap is;
- $S_{low}$: how absolutely low actual throughput is;
- $S_{drop}$: how severe the temporal/local drop is.

RB-efficiency severity can also raise the magnitude floor.

This is superior to binary counting because:

```text
actual 1 Mbps vs expected 100 Mbps
```

should be much more severe than:

```text
actual 14 Mbps vs expected 30 Mbps
```

even if both trigger the same nominal detector.

---

# 28. Low-tail consensus as bounded evidence

The agreement among global P10, IQR, and robust MAD contributes a small bounded bonus.

Conceptually:

```text
0 methods agree → no low-tail consensus bonus
2 methods agree → supporting evidence
3 methods agree → strongest bounded low-tail consensus
```

The maximum bonus is deliberately small relative to the main magnitude score.

This retains all three methods while preventing correlated double/triple counting.

---

# 29. Independent-family bonus

A small bonus is added when more than one independent family supports the anomaly.

The bonus grows sublinearly, approximately with a logarithmic form and a cap.

This means:

```text
LOW + TEMPORAL
```

is more convincing than LOW alone,

and:

```text
LOW + TEMPORAL + EXPECTED + RB_EFFICIENCY
```

is stronger still,

but the score cannot explode simply because many flags are true.

---

# 30. Context/evidence bonus

Small bonuses may be contributed by evidence such as:

- low-tail consensus;
- confirmed high RB demand;
- CA activity;
- nearby relevant events;
- poor-radio context.

The total context bonus is capped.

This enforces the correct hierarchy:

> **Actual anomaly magnitude should dominate. Context should support the interpretation, not manufacture the anomaly.**

---

# 31. Low-RB uncertainty handling

If reliable RB evidence indicates low allocation/demand and there is no strong independent radio/temporal explanation, the score is softened.

Why?

Low throughput under low resource usage may reflect low offered load rather than a network inability to deliver throughput.

The notebook uses a smooth cap/compression rather than a crude hard reset so the anomaly evidence is not discarded completely.

---

# 32. High-score soft cap and catastrophic 100

Non-catastrophic high scores are smoothly compressed near the top of the 0–100 range.

This prevents too many ordinary anomalies from clustering at 95–100.

A true 100 is reserved for very strict catastrophic combinations, such as extremely low actual throughput, very high expected opportunity, sufficient resource evidence, and multiple independent detector families.

This improves ranking interpretability.

---

# 33. Statistical RCA handoff gate

Not every statistically flagged row is sent to RCA.

The notebook distinguishes:

```text
broad candidate detection
```

from:

```text
practical RCA handoff eligibility
```

A baseline statistical handoff generally requires a meaningful score and useful evidence such as:

- expected-underperformance;
- RB-efficiency;
- radio-limited context;
- temporal collapse;
- sustained degradation.

Weak low-tail-only findings, especially under uncertain/low demand, can remain audit candidates without flooding the RCA team.

---

# 34. Fusion/threshold diagnostics before ML

The notebook creates empirical audits such as:

- statistical family counts;
- detector overlap;
- low-tail overlap;
- score-component contribution;
- bonus contribution versus base/magnitude.

These plots/tables answer:

> “Are we detecting genuinely different anomaly mechanisms, or just counting the same low-throughput fact repeatedly?”

and:

> “Are context bonuses becoming more important than the actual anomaly?”

This is exactly how the statistical outputs are used to validate fusion design without overfitting weights automatically.

---

# 35. Worst-performing cell / PCI ranking

The notebook adds a separate **population-level performance ranking**.

This is not the same as row-level anomaly detection.

The goal is:

> “Which serving cells consistently look worst across the drive test, and what KPIs explain why?”

---

# 36. Cell identity strategy

Primary grouping prefers a unique serving-cell identity such as ECI when available.

If ECI is unavailable, a composite such as:

```text
EARFCN + PCI
```

is safer than PCI alone.

A separate PCI-only ranking is still exported, but it carries a reuse caution because the same PCI may be reused by different cells.

The ranking requires at least approximately 30 rows per entity. At least one observed session is required by the current code, while a separate reliability label gives stronger confidence to entities supported by more rows/sessions.

---

# 37. Cell-level aggregated KPIs

For each cell/entity, the ranking aggregates measures such as:

- median throughput;
- P10 throughput;
- mean throughput;
- anomaly rate;
- RCA-handoff rate;
- median/P90 expected-throughput gap;
- median RSRP;
- median RSRQ;
- median SINR;
- median CQI;
- median MCS;
- median BLER and high-tail BLER;
- median RB usage;
- median throughput per RB;
- number of rows;
- number of sessions.

This gives RCA both the rank and the reasons behind it.

---

# 38. Data-driven badness percentiles

Instead of imposing one universal “bad cell” threshold, each metric is percentile-ranked across cells.

For a metric where **low is bad**, such as median throughput, CQI, MCS, or SINR, a lower value receives a higher badness percentile.

For a metric where **high is bad**, such as anomaly rate, expected-throughput gap, or BLER, a higher value receives a higher badness percentile.

A weighted score combines available components.

The implemented conceptual weighting emphasizes roughly:

```text
median throughput       25%
P10 throughput           12%
anomaly rate             13%
median P75 gap            10%
CQI                       10%
MCS                        8%
BLER                      10%
SINR                       6%
RSRQ                       4%
RSRP                       2%
```

Weights are normalized over the metrics actually available for that entity.

This is a strong design because poor throughput remains the main objective, while radio/link-quality KPIs explain *why* the cell ranks badly.

---

# 39. Bad-cell reliability label

Ranking evidence is more trustworthy when a cell is observed many times and across multiple sessions.

The notebook assigns reliability approximately as:

```text
HIGH   → ≥100 rows and ≥3 sessions
MEDIUM → ≥50 rows and ≥2 sessions
LOW    → otherwise, after minimum eligibility
```

Therefore RCA should not interpret:

```text
Rank 1 with 31 rows from one short session
```

as equally reliable as:

```text
Rank 2 with 2,000 rows across 10 sessions.
```

The top 25 cell and PCI views are exported for handoff/review.

---

# 40. ML philosophy

The ML layer is not allowed to become the main opaque anomaly detector.

Instead, ML asks:

> “Given this sample’s legitimate context, what throughput would a learned model expect?”

Then a row is suspicious when actual throughput is far below the learned expectation.

This makes ML evidence interpretable through:

```text
expected throughput
actual throughput
absolute gap
actual/expected ratio
residual percentile
```

rather than only an abstract anomaly score.

---

# 41. Strict causal forecasting rule

For any ML predictor at time \(t\), the feature must be knowable at or before \(t\).

The notebook explicitly excludes:

- symmetric ±event windows;
- anomaly labels/scores;
- expected-throughput fields generated later;
- target-derived gaps/ratios/residuals;
- centered rolling anomaly outputs;
- future-looking features.

Past-only event counts such as `*_count_prevXs` are allowed.

Past throughput and KPI lags are created strictly within session boundaries.

---

# 42. Causal lag features

The strict temporal feature view includes past throughput information such as:

```text
lag 1 second
lag 2 seconds
lag 3 seconds
lag 5 seconds
lag 10 seconds
past-only median over 5 samples
past-only mean over 10 samples
past trend/slope
history count
```

It also uses past-only versions of technical KPIs, for example lag/rolling-past context for:

- RSRP;
- SINR;
- RB usage;
- BLER;
- CQI.

All are grouped by session before shifting, so the end of one drive-test session cannot leak into the beginning of another.

---

# 43. Resource-conditioned production feature view

The resource model uses current legitimate technical context such as:

- radio KPIs;
- carrier aggregation/carrier count;
- band/EARFCN;
- bandwidth;
- RB/resource demand;
- CQI;
- MCS;
- BLER;
- rank;
- SCell context;
- causal past-event features;
- selected categorical context.

It does not use current target-derived anomaly outputs.

Its purpose is:

> “Given the current radio/resource configuration, what throughput is normally expected?”

---

# 44. HGB resource-conditioned central/Q50-labelled model

The production resource model uses `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` on a transformed target:

$$
y=\ln(1+Throughput)
$$

The prediction is transformed back with `expm1`.

The notebook names the model as a Q50/central expected-throughput model.

## Important exact implementation nuance

The current HGB configuration uses the regressor’s ordinary squared-error objective rather than an explicit quantile loss.

Therefore, mathematically, it is best understood as a **central conditional prediction in log-throughput space**, not a strict median-quantile estimator in the same sense as the LSTM pinball-loss Q50 model.

The “Q50” naming is a project convention for the central expected reference.

This distinction is worth knowing when presenting the model accurately.

## Why HGB?

Histogram Gradient Boosting:

1. bins continuous features into histograms;
2. builds decision trees iteratively;
3. each new tree fits remaining prediction error;
4. nonlinear interactions are learned automatically;
5. trees can model effects such as “high BLER only becomes strongly harmful when SINR is also poor.”

Typical configured parameters include hundreds of boosting iterations, a small learning rate, controlled leaf size, and regularization.

---

# 45. HGB strict-causal temporal model

The temporal HGB model uses the resource features plus past-only history features.

It asks:

> “Based on the technical context and what throughput/KPIs were doing in previous seconds, what should throughput be now?”

This model is particularly useful for detecting local temporal breaks.

Example:

```text
Past 10 seconds: 70–85 Mbps
Current conditions remain similar
Model expects ~75 Mbps
Actual suddenly becomes 12 Mbps
```

That creates strong causal temporal underperformance evidence.

Because only past samples are used, the model can legitimately be described as a causal/offline-forecasting style model with respect to temporal feature construction.

---

# 46. True Q75 resource quantile model

The upper expected-throughput model uses a Gradient Boosting Regressor with quantile loss and:

$$
\alpha=0.75
$$

This is a true conditional 75th-percentile objective.

Instead of minimizing squared error, quantile loss penalizes under- and over-prediction asymmetrically so the fitted function estimates:

$$
Q_{0.75}(T\mid X)
$$

where \(X\) is the resource/radio feature context.

This model answers:

> “Under these conditions, what level is near the upper-performing quartile?”

It is particularly useful for calculating opportunity gaps.

---

# 47. Why use both central and Q75 ML expectations

The models answer different questions.

## Central resource model

> “What throughput is typical/centrally expected?”

## Q75 resource model

> “What throughput do relatively strong performers achieve under similar conditions?”

If actual throughput is far below both, the anomaly claim is stronger.

The Q75 model is especially useful when actual throughput might look modestly low relative to the center but extremely poor relative to achievable opportunity.

---

# 48. Group-aware cross-validation by session

Random row splitting would be unsafe.

Adjacent drive-test samples are highly correlated. If second 100 is in training and second 101 from the same session is in test, the test metric can look unrealistically good.

The notebook therefore uses **group-aware splitting**, where the session identifier is the group.

Conceptually:

```text
Training fold: entire sessions A, B, C, D
Test fold:     entire session E
```

No session is divided between train and test in a fold.

This is one of the strongest protections against temporal/session leakage.

---

# 49. Out-of-fold predictions

For every row, the notebook aims to generate an expected-throughput prediction from a model that did **not** train on that row’s session.

These are out-of-fold (OOF) predictions.

Why use OOF predictions for anomaly detection?

If a model predicts training rows using a model trained on those same rows, it may overfit and make residuals artificially small.

OOF residuals are much more honest:

$$
Residual_i = Actual_i - OOFexpected_i
$$

A very negative residual means actual throughput is much worse than the model expected on unseen-session behavior.

---

# 50. Nested robust reference fitting

An anomaly model has a subtle problem:

If severe low-throughput anomalies are included as ordinary training examples, the expected-throughput model may learn that bad performance is “normal.”

The notebook addresses this using a nested robust-reference procedure inside each outer training fold.

Conceptually:

1. Use only outer-training sessions.
2. Produce inner OOF predictions within that training population.
3. Compute residuals.
4. Identify the extreme negative residual tail, approximately the lowest 1%.
5. Remove those most extreme underperformers from reference-model refitting.
6. Fit the final outer-fold expected-throughput model on the cleaned reference population.
7. Predict the untouched outer test sessions.

Crucially, the outer test fold is never used to decide which training rows are removed.

This makes the expected baseline less likely to normalize the very failures it is supposed to detect.

---

# 51. Q75 fold-wise calibration

The Q75 model is calibrated within training folds so its empirical coverage is closer to 75%.

Conceptually, if inner-training OOF predictions systematically underpredict the true upper quantile, a correction is learned from the training residual distribution and applied to the outer-fold model.

The calibration target is approximately:

$$
P(T \le \hat Q_{0.75})\approx 0.75
$$

Calibration is checked by prediction decile and technical condition.

This is important because a Q75 model that only covers 55% of observations is not actually functioning as an upper-quartile reference.

---

# 52. Model preprocessing inside folds

Numeric and categorical features are processed with training-fold-only transformations.

Typical behavior includes:

- median imputation for numeric fields;
- most-frequent imputation for categorical fields;
- one-hot encoding for categorical variables;
- handling unknown categories safely.

The critical point is that learned preprocessing statistics are fitted on training data, not on the complete dataset before cross-validation.

---

# 53. ML evaluation metrics

The notebook reports metrics such as:

## MAE

$$
MAE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i |y_i-\hat y_i|
$$

This answers:

> “On average, how many Mbps is the prediction off by?”

## RMSE

$$
RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}
$$

This penalizes large errors more heavily.

## R²

$$
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}
$$

Higher is better. It describes variance explained relative to predicting the mean.

## Q75 pinball loss and coverage

These validate the quantile model specifically.

A Q75 model should not be judged only by R² because its objective is intentionally asymmetric.

---

# 54. Train vs held-out test plots

The optimized notebook explicitly generates plots where training and held-out test performance are shown on the same figure with distinct visual groups.

These plots help answer:

- Does the model fit training data much better than unseen sessions?
- Is there obvious overfitting?
- Are predictions biased at low or high throughput?
- Does the model preserve the correct trend on test sessions?

The plot also displays train/test metrics so model quality is visually and numerically auditable.

A single representative fold is used for direct train-vs-test visual comparison, while OOF/repeated validation metrics summarize broader generalization.

# 55. LSTM Q50 validation model

The notebook retains an LSTM as a validation model.

It does **not** contribute to final RCA anomaly decisions.

## 55.1 Sequence construction

With the one-second sampling interval and a sequence length around 20, each prediction uses approximately the previous 20 seconds.

Sequences never cross session boundaries.

The target sample itself is not included in the historical input sequence.

## 55.2 Inputs

The LSTM sequence includes past technical features and a standardized past-throughput channel.

## 55.3 Architecture

A typical structure is:

```text
multivariate sequence
    ↓
LSTM hidden representation
    ↓
dropout / dense head
    ↓
Q50 throughput prediction
```

## 55.4 Loss

The LSTM uses Q50 pinball loss.

For quantile \(q=0.5\), pinball loss is:

$$
L_{0.5}(y,\hat y)=
\begin{cases}
0.5(y-\hat y), & y\ge\hat y\\
0.5(\hat y-y), & y<\hat y
\end{cases}
$$

At q=0.5 this corresponds to median-oriented absolute-error behavior.

## 55.5 Why keep it only for validation?

The project wants to prove that the simpler HGB models were selected because of better practical predictive/anomaly performance, not because sequence models were ignored.

The LSTM adds:

- more preprocessing complexity;
- sequence coverage loss for the first history window of each session;
- more training complexity;
- potentially weaker held-out accuracy in this dataset.

It therefore remains a model-selection comparator and context model.

---

# 56. Isolation Forest validation/context model

Isolation Forest is an unsupervised anomaly method.

Its core idea is:

> Anomalous points are easier to isolate using random feature splits, so they tend to require shorter tree paths.

The notebook uses many randomized isolation trees with a small assumed contamination rate, around 3%.

The feature context can include throughput, model residuals/expectations, and technical KPIs.

Because it may use the actual target and derived residual context, it is **not a causal throughput forecast**.

It is retained to answer:

> “Does an unsupervised multivariate detector also consider these cases unusual?”

It is intentionally excluded from final anomaly cases and final RCA JSON.

# 57. Production vs validation-only ML models

## Production ML evidence

The final fusion uses three expected-throughput views:

1. HGB resource-conditioned central/Q50-labelled expectation;
2. HGB strict-causal temporal central/Q50-labelled expectation;
3. calibrated resource-conditioned Q75 quantile expectation.

## Validation/context only

- LSTM Q50;
- Isolation Forest.

The notebook produces model-comparison plots/tables explaining this keep/drop/context decision.

---

# 58. ML expected-throughput gaps and ratios

For model \(m\):

$$
E_{i,m}=\text{OOF expected throughput}
$$

$$
Gap_{i,m}=E_{i,m}-T_i
$$

$$
Ratio_{i,m}=\frac{T_i}{E_{i,m}}
$$

A negative residual in log space is also computed:

$$
r_{i,m}=\ln(1+T_i)-\ln(1+E_{i,m})
$$

The residual distribution is converted to percentiles so the algorithm can determine whether a point lies in an unusually negative tail.

---

# 59. Strict ML underperformance rule

A production model does not flag a row merely because the prediction error is nonzero.

The strict logic combines:

1. meaningful expected opportunity;
2. a large absolute gap;
3. a poor actual/expected ratio;
4. an unusually negative OOF residual tail;
5. resource-demand confirmation where appropriate.

Typical gates include:

```text
expected ≥ 25 Mbps
expected - actual ≥ 15 Mbps
actual / expected ≤ model-specific gate
residual ≤ model OOF lower-tail threshold
```

The resource/Q75 ratio gate is stricter, around 0.40. The temporal central model uses a somewhat looser ratio gate around 0.50 because its role differs.

A severe ML condition requires an even more extreme residual tail and stronger gap/low-throughput combination.

---

# 60. Exact note about the “magnitude evidence count” wording

The executable final code uses the strict joint conditions described above: gap/ratio/opportunity plus residual-tail confirmation.

A retained configuration/comment mentions a historical “minimum magnitude evidence count” concept, but it is no longer the main executable decision rule.

When explaining or presenting the final notebook, the code behavior should take precedence:

> **ML anomaly evidence is conjunctive and strict, not simply “two out of three loose conditions.”**

---

# 61. ML evidence score

For each production model, a continuous evidence strength combines dimensions such as:

- ratio severity;
- absolute gap severity;
- residual-tail extremeness.

A simplified form is approximately:

$$
Evidence_m=
0.35S_{ratio}
+0.35S_{gap}
+0.30S_{tail}
$$

where each component is normalized to 0–1.

This produces a more meaningful confidence value than a binary flag alone.

---

# 62. ML family abstraction

The production models are grouped into two independent ML families.

## Resource family

Supported by:

- resource-conditioned central HGB;
- resource-conditioned Q75 model.

## Temporal family

Supported by:

- strict-causal temporal HGB.

The LSTM is not counted as a third production family, and Isolation Forest is not counted as a production family.

This prevents model proliferation from inflating confidence.

---

# 63. Reliability priors

Different ML models/families receive different reliability priors based on their role and predictive quality.

The production HGB temporal/resource and Q75 models receive higher reliability weighting than the LSTM and Isolation Forest context models.

Conceptually:

```text
strong validated production predictor → high reliability prior
validation-only sequence model         → lower prior
unsupervised context model             → lower prior
```

Only production-family evidence enters final fusion.

---

# 64. ML confidence via noisy-OR

Resource and temporal evidence are combined using a probabilistic-style noisy-OR form:

$$
C_{ML}=1-(1-E_{resource})(1-E_{temporal})
$$

This has useful behavior:

- one strong family can create meaningful confidence;
- two strong independent families increase confidence;
- confidence remains bounded at 1.

Small bonuses may be added for both families agreeing and for reliable medium/high RB demand.

---

# 65. ML impact score

ML impact is distinct from ML confidence.

Impact uses the practical seriousness of the degradation, based on dimensions such as:

- absolute low throughput;
- expected-minus-actual gap;
- actual/expected ratio.

A representative form is:

$$
Impact_{ML}=100(0.25S_{low}+0.40S_{gap}+0.35S_{ratio})
$$

The final ML score then combines impact and confidence approximately as:

$$
Score_{ML}=0.55Impact_{ML}+0.45Confidence_{ML}
$$

This mirrors the broader pipeline philosophy:

> “How bad is the performance?” and “How sure are we?” are different questions.

---

# 66. Strict ML-only anomaly retention

The final design intentionally allows a **small number of ML-only anomalies**.

However, they must be strict.

A final ML anomaly generally requires either:

- agreement from both independent production ML families, or
- an exceptionally strong single-family case with stringent confidence, gap, ratio, and evidence thresholds.

The strong-single path uses very strict conditions on the order of:

```text
confidence ≥ 80
absolute gap ≥ 40 Mbps
actual/expected ≤ 0.20
model evidence ≥ 0.80
```

This keeps ML-only anomalies rare and high-value.

---

# 67. Human-review requirement for ML-only RCA cases

An ML-only incident can still be handed to RCA, but it is explicitly marked:

```text
requires_human_review = true
human_review_reason = strict_ml_only_anomaly_requires_supervised_verification
```

This is a strong governance choice.

It acknowledges:

> “The ML evidence is strong enough to investigate, but there is not yet independent statistical handoff support, so a human should confirm the case.”

The case remains operationally useful without overstating certainty.

---

# 68. Statistical–ML fusion philosophy

The fusion is not:

```text
stat score + ML score = final score
```

Instead, it separates:

1. **impact** — how serious the throughput degradation is;
2. **confidence** — how much independent evidence supports that interpretation;
3. **family mechanism** — resource, temporal, radio, etc.;
4. **handoff logic** — whether the case is useful enough for RCA.

This makes the score more explainable.

---

# 69. Statistical evidence reliability

Statistical detections receive reliability factors based on the type of evidence.

Examples conceptually:

```text
reliable P75 / RB efficiency / strict CA → very high reliability
radio-limited context                    → high
strong temporal evidence                 → high
sustained degradation                    → moderate-high
low-tail-only evidence                   → lower
```

Statistical evidence strength is approximately:

$$
E_{stat}=\frac{StatScore}{100}\times Reliability_{stat}
$$'

This prevents a low-tail-only score from being treated as equally trustworthy as a highly reliable expected-underperformance case with confirmed demand.

---

# 70. Fused detection confidence

The notebook combines statistical and production-ML evidence using a bounded noisy-OR-like structure.

A simplified representation of the implemented logic is:

$$
C_{fused}=1-(1-0.80E_{stat})(1-0.90E_{ML})
+Bonuses
$$

Small bounded bonuses can reflect:

- statistical–ML agreement;
- confirmed medium/high RB demand;
- support from both ML production families.

The result is clipped to a valid confidence range.

The coefficients intentionally keep statistical evidence central while allowing high-quality ML agreement to strengthen confidence.

---

# 71. Fused impact families

The fusion calculates impact through interpretable mechanism families rather than one undifferentiated expected-throughput number.

## Resource impact

Uses resource-conditioned expectations such as:

- RB-conditioned statistical P75;
- ML resource Q75;
- ML resource central expectation.

## Temporal impact

Uses:

- causal temporal HGB expectation;
- local rolling median context;
- explicit local/previous drop magnitude.

## Capability/expected impact

Uses reliable radio-conditioned statistical P75 context. Some internal legacy naming uses “capability” terminology even though the final production ML capability model was removed.

## Radio impact

Uses actual low-throughput seriousness and persistence in radio-limited cases rather than pretending radio failures have a clean expected-throughput predictor.

The dominant active family determines the most relevant impact interpretation.

---

# 72. Fused priority score

The final continuous priority follows the same project-wide balance:

$$
Priority=0.55\times Impact+0.45\times Confidence
$$

Why slightly favor impact?

Because RCA capacity should be spent primarily on cases that materially hurt user throughput, while confidence determines how strongly the system believes the evidence.

A very confident but tiny deviation should not outrank a catastrophic throughput collapse with slightly less detector agreement.

# 73. Row-level final handoff paths

The notebook defines several legitimate ways for a row to become final RCA evidence.

## 73.1 Statistical + ML consensus

Strongest general case: both statistical handoff evidence and strict production ML evidence agree.

## 73.2 Statistical expected/resource case

Reliable P75, RB-efficiency, or CA-type evidence can be handed off even without ML if priority is high enough.

## 73.3 Statistical radio case

Radio-limited cases can be retained when sufficiently severe/persistent.

## 73.4 Statistical temporal case

Large local/sudden drops can be retained when the temporal drop magnitude and priority are strong enough.

## 73.5 Strict ML-only case

Allowed only under strict criteria and marked for human review.

This is more robust than requiring every anomaly to satisfy exactly the same detector combination.

# 75. Why final row anomalies are not sent directly to RCA

A one-second drive-test dataset can generate many consecutive anomalous rows from one real network incident.

Sending every row would produce:

```text

```

# 76. Anomaly episode construction

Within each session, final anomaly rows are sorted by time.

A new episode begins when continuity breaks, for example when:

- timestamp gap exceeds a small episode limit;
- original `source_index` is no longer consecutive.

Because the verified sampling is one second, this makes consecutive anomalous samples naturally form one episode.

The `source_index` check is important: if only anomalous rows are filtered, two anomaly timestamps may look adjacent even though normal rows existed between them. Source-index continuity prevents false merging.

Episode-level impact combines dimensions such as:

- maximum row impact;

- duration.

A representative implemented weighting is roughly:

20% mean row impact
15% persistence
10% duration
```

This means a long moderate degradation and a short catastrophic collapse can both rank meaningfully, but for different reasons.

# 78. Episode confidence

Episode confidence similarly combines:

- maximum row confidence;
- mean row confidence;
- fraction/amount of consensus evidence.

A representative weighting is approximately:

15% agreement/consensus
```

The episode priority then again balances impact and confidence.

# 80. Operational incident consolidation

# 89. How to interpret train vs test accuracy

Suppose a model shows:

```text
Train R² = 0.99
Test R² = 0.70
```

This suggests overfitting or poor session generalization.

A healthier pattern is:

```text
Train R² high
Test R² also high and reasonably close
```

Similarly, MAE should not explode on held-out sessions.

Because the split is session-aware, test performance has stronger meaning than a random-row split.

# 90. Q75 calibration interpretation

For a true Q75 predictor, approximately 75% of actual observations should be at or below the predicted Q75 over a well-matched population.

Coverage by decile and condition helps detect failure modes such as:

- systematic underprediction in high-throughput regions;
- overprediction in poor-radio conditions;
- unstable behavior for rare bands/EARFCNs;
- calibration drift across RB-demand buckets.

A Q75 model can have decent average error yet poor quantile calibration, so both must be reviewed.

# 91. End-to-end worked example

Assume one sample has:

```text
Actual throughput = 5 Mbps
Radio-conditioned P75 = 55 Mbps
RB-conditioned P75 = 65 Mbps
HGB resource expectation = 58 Mbps
ML resource Q75 = 72 Mbps
HGB causal temporal expectation = 62 Mbps
Previous/local throughput ≈ 75 Mbps
RB demand = high
RSRP = -91 dBm
SINR = 17 dB
CQI = 13
MCS = 22
BLER = 2%
```

## Step 1 — low-tail statistics

5 Mbps may trigger:

```text
fixed low
P10
possibly IQR
possibly robust MAD
```

These remain visible, but P10/IQR/MAD are grouped as one low-tail family with bounded consensus support.

## Step 2 — temporal evidence

The drop from around 75 to 5 Mbps creates:

```text
large local gap
very poor local ratio
possibly sudden one-second drop
```

Temporal family = active.

## Step 3 — expected-throughput evidence

For RB P75:

\[
Gap=65-5=60\text{ Mbps}
\]

\[
Ratio=5/65\approx0.077
\]

This is far below strict underperformance gates.

## Step 4 — RB efficiency

If high RB usage is present but throughput is only 5 Mbps, throughput-per-RB may be in its lower tail.

RB-efficiency family may activate.

## Step 5 — radio interpretation

RSRP, SINR, CQI, MCS, and BLER are relatively favorable.

Therefore the case is not easily explained by obvious poor radio.

This increases RCA interest in resource/scheduler/CA/transport/application/configuration mechanisms.

## Step 6 — ML evidence

All three production expectations are far above actual throughput.

OOF residuals are likely extremely negative.

Resource and temporal ML families may both activate.

## Step 7 — fusion

Impact is very high because:

- actual is extremely low;
- expected gap is large;
- actual/expected ratio is tiny;
- local drop is large.

Confidence is very high because:

- multiple independent statistical families agree;
- production ML agrees;
- RB demand confirms opportunity.

The incident therefore receives a high/critical priority.

## Step 8 — RCA JSON

The RCA team receives a compact record showing:

```text
5-Mbps actual
~60-Mbps expected gap
strong CQI/MCS, low BLER
high RB demand
cell bad-rank context
statistical + ML consensus
specific detection case
```

This is actionable without exporting 600+ columns.

# 94. How the statistical methods validate thresholds and fusion weights

The robust distribution-derived methods are particularly useful for calibration review.

## Threshold validation

Compare:

```text
fixed 10 Mbps
Global P10 cutoff
IQR implied cutoff
MAD implied cutoff
Session P10 distribution
```

If the empirical cutoffs cluster near the fixed threshold, the fixed anchor is supported by the data.

If they differ greatly, that does not automatically mean the fixed threshold is wrong, but it tells the analyst that its role must be interpreted carefully.

## Fusion validation

Examine:

- how many rows each method flags;
- pairwise overlap;
- family counts;
- score distributions by detector type;
- contribution of base/magnitude vs bonuses;
- final incident composition.

A healthy fusion should not produce high scores mainly because three correlated low-tail methods all fired.

The optimized notebook’s grouped low-tail consensus is designed specifically to prevent that.

# 95. What “statistical-first” means operationally

It does not mean ML is ignored.

It means:

1. The anomaly population is fundamentally explainable through transparent statistical mechanisms.
2. ML adds context-specific expected-throughput validation.
3. ML can strengthen confidence and discover a very small strict ML-only set.
4. Final RCA evidence remains interpretable without requiring the RCA engineer to trust a black-box score.

The intended final pattern is therefore:

```text
Statistical evidence → primary detection foundation
ML expected throughput → validation / confidence / strict discoveries
Fusion → prioritization
RCA JSON → compact technical evidence
```

# 96. Key implementation cautions to remember when presenting

## 96.1 HGB “Q50” naming

The HGB central models are labeled Q50 in project terminology, but the executable regressor uses squared-error fitting in log space, not explicit quantile loss.

Describe them as **central expected-throughput HGB models** unless you later change the loss to an actual quantile objective.

The LSTM Q50 and GradientBoosting Q75 are true quantile-oriented models because they use pinball/quantile loss.

## 96.2 Centered rolling statistics are offline

The 11-sample centered rolling detector may use future neighboring samples.

It is valid for offline anomaly detection, but it must not be described as causal forecasting.

## 96.3 Symmetric event windows are RCA-only

`*_pm*` or generic symmetric event proximity fields are not ML predictors.

## 96.4 PCI-only rank can mix reused PCIs

Primary bad-cell ranking should be preferred over PCI-only ranking whenever ECI/composite identity is available.

## 96.5 Detection case is not confirmed root cause

A label such as `radio_limited`, `CA_underperformance`, or `RB_efficiency` describes the detection evidence and likely investigation direction. It is not a final RCA conclusion until the RCA team validates it.

# 99. Final conceptual summary

The notebook can be summarized as five layers.

## Layer 1 — “Is throughput objectively low or degraded?”

Use robust statistics:

```text
fixed threshold
P10
session P10
IQR
MAD
rolling/local drop
sudden drop
```

## Layer 2 — “Is it low relative to what conditions suggest should be achievable?”

Use:

```text
radio-conditioned P75
RB-conditioned P75
RB efficiency
CA context
```

## Layer 3 — “Do learned causal expectations agree?”

Use production ML:

```text
HGB resource central expectation
HGB causal temporal central expectation
calibrated resource Q75
```

Validate model choice using:

```text
LSTM Q50
Isolation Forest
```

without letting them pollute final RCA evidence.

## Layer 4 — “How important and trustworthy is the anomaly?”

Fuse:

```text
impact
confidence
independent families
resource-demand confirmation
statistical–ML agreement
```

## Layer 5 — “What should RCA actually receive?”

Convert:

```text
rows
→ episodes
→ strict episodes
→ operational incidents
→ diverse shortlist
→ compact JSON + bad-cell rankings + explanatory plots
```

The final architecture is intentionally designed so that every high-priority incident can be explained in plain technical language:

> **What happened to throughput, how large was the gap/drop, which independent detectors agreed, what did the causal models expect, what did the radio/resource KPIs show, how bad is this serving cell historically, and why is this incident worth RCA attention?**

That is the core logic of the optimized final anomaly-detection notebook.
