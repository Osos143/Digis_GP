# 2. Plot adjustment: removing the active-download-window marker

## What changed

The previous throughput context graph placed `x` markers at the bottom of the graph for rows outside the parsed active download window.

That marker has been removed from anomaly context plots.

## Why it was removed

The active-download window is no longer the main anomaly-analysis mask. The finalized logic uses all valid LTE downlink throughput rows because the earlier analysis found that active, warm-up, cool-down, and outside-window throughput distributions were sufficiently similar for the current data.

Therefore, marking every outside-window sample in the main anomaly graph added visual noise without changing the anomaly interpretation.

The download-window fields are still retained as contextual data when needed. They are simply not drawn on the primary throughput graph.

## Result

The throughput plot now focuses on:

- actual throughput;
- statistical expected throughput;
- ML expected throughput;
- rolling median;
- selected anomaly time;
- complete anomaly episode.

# 3. Radio plots now include CQI, MCS, and BLER

The previous radio plot combined only:

- RSRP;
- RSRQ;
- SINR.

The updated visualization uses two vertically aligned panels.

## Panel 1: signal and interference evidence

This panel contains:

- **RSRP**: received signal strength;
- **RSRQ**: received signal quality including interference and loading effects;
- **SINR**: useful signal relative to interference and noise.

This panel is useful for identifying:

- weak coverage;
- poor dominance;
- interference;
- coverage-edge behavior;
- mobility-related radio degradation.

## Panel 2: link adaptation and decoding evidence

This panel contains:

- **CQI**;
- **MCS**;
- **BLER**.

CQI and MCS are plotted against the main left axis. BLER uses a separate right axis because its scale can differ materially from CQI and MCS.

### Why these KPIs are important

A throughput anomaly may occur with acceptable RSRP but still show poor radio efficiency. For example:

- SINR falls because of interference;
- CQI falls;
- the scheduler selects a lower MCS;
- BLER rises;
- retransmissions increase;
- throughput collapses.

Viewing CQI, MCS, and BLER together makes this chain far easier to validate.

## Episode visibility

Both radio panels include:

- a dashed vertical line at the selected anomaly;
- a shaded interval covering the complete detected episode;
- dotted episode-boundary lines.

This makes it possible to see whether the radio degradation occurred:

- before the throughput anomaly;
- during the anomaly episode;
- after the throughput recovered.

# 4. Training and held-out predictive-fit plots

## What was requested

A graph similar to the representative held-out-session prediction plot was requested for all predictive methods, showing:

1. training-data fit;
2. held-out predictive-data fit;
3. R² at the top;
4. MAE at the top.

## What was implemented

Each predictive method now receives a two-panel figure:

- **left panel:** representative training session;
- **right panel:** representative completely held-out session.

The figure title reports metrics calculated over the complete comparison split, not only the representative session shown in the panel.

The plots are generated for:

1. HGB strict-causal temporal central predictor;
2. HGB resource-conditioned central predictor;
3. calibrated resource-conditioned Q75 predictor;
4. LSTM-Q50 validation comparator.

## Why a representative session is used

A scatter plot is useful for overall predictive agreement, but it hides temporal behavior.

A session-order line plot shows whether the model can follow:

- rapid throughput increases;
- rapid drops;
- sustained low-throughput periods;
- recovery periods;
- transitions between high and low throughput.

The complete-split R² and MAE remain in the title so the graph does not rely on one visually selected session.

## R² interpretation

For true values \(y_i\), predictions \(\hat y_i\), and mean target \(\bar y\):

\[
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}
\]

Interpretation:

- \(R^2=1\): perfect fit;
- \(R^2=0\): no better than predicting the mean;
- \(R^2<0\): worse than the mean baseline.

A large difference between training R² and held-out R² can indicate overfitting.

## MAE interpretation

\[
MAE = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |y_i-\hat y_i|
\]

MAE is directly interpretable in Mbps.

For example, an MAE of 8 Mbps means the model prediction differs from the actual throughput by about 8 Mbps on average.

## Important note for Q75

R² and MAE are shown for Q75 because they help the reviewer understand its numerical distance from the actual values. However, Q75 is not intended to predict the center of the distribution.

The primary Q75 validation measures remain:

- empirical 75% coverage;
- pinball loss;
- calibration by prediction decile.

A well-calibrated Q75 should satisfy approximately:

\[
P(Y \leq \hat Q_{0.75}) \approx 0.75
\]

## Important note for Isolation Forest

Isolation Forest does not predict throughput. Therefore, R² and MAE are not mathematically applicable.

It receives a separate out-of-fold anomaly-strength distribution plot showing:

- rows not isolated by the model;
- rows isolated as unusual.

It remains contextual validation only.

# 6. Preventing anomaly graphs from starting at the anomaly

## Problem

Some context graphs placed the selected anomaly at the extreme left of the x-axis. This prevented visual verification of what happened before the anomaly.

## Updated rule

A candidate is plotted only when at least 15 seconds of real pre-anomaly history exist in the same session.

Because the verified sample interval is exactly one second, this corresponds to at least 15 preceding samples.

The context x-axis is now expressed as seconds relative to the selected anomaly or episode start:

- negative values: before the anomaly;
- zero: selected anomaly or episode start;
- positive values: after the anomaly.

## Candidate selection

When the highest-ranked row is too close to the beginning of a session, the notebook skips that row and searches for the next high-priority candidate in the same anomaly case that has enough preceding context.

This avoids presenting an uninformative graph merely because the first row had the highest score.

# 7. Showing the anomaly episode on every context graph

## What changed

Context plots now show both:

- the selected anomaly row;
- the entire anomaly episode.

The selected row is a dashed vertical line. The episode is a shaded area with visible start and end boundaries.

## How the episode is located for plotting

The plot function starts at the selected anomaly and expands backward and forward while:

1. neighboring rows remain anomaly rows;
2. rows remain in the same session;
3. the gap between consecutive timestamps does not exceed the episode-gap limit.

The current row-level statistical episode gap allows up to three seconds between retained anomaly rows. Final fused episode plots use the stored final episode ID directly.

## Why the selected row and episode are both required

The selected row usually represents the strongest point inside the incident. The episode represents the period affected.

Without the selected row, the reviewer cannot see the exact ranked sample.

Without the episode span, the reviewer cannot distinguish:

- one isolated bad second;
- a five-second degradation;
- a long sustained throughput collapse.

# 8. What do ratio, gap, low, and drop compare against?

The continuous magnitude score uses four interpretable dimensions:

\[
Magnitude = 100\left(0.35S_{ratio}+0.25S_{gap}+0.25S_{low}+0.15S_{drop}\right)
\]

The updated notebook now exports the exact reference source used for each row.

## 8.1 Ratio severity

\[
ratio = \frac{actual}{expected}
\]

In the statistical score, `expected` is the larger reliable value from:

- radio-condition P75;
- RB-conditioned P75.

The larger expected value produces the smaller actual/expected ratio and the larger performance deficit. Both ratio and gap therefore use one consistent expected reference.

The normalized ratio severity is:

\[
S_{ratio} = clip\left(\frac{0.60-ratio}{0.60},0,1\right)
\]

Examples:

- actual/expected = 0.60 → ratio severity = 0;
- actual/expected = 0.30 → ratio severity = 0.50;
- actual/expected = 0.00 → ratio severity = 1.

The 0.60 value is a severity normalization reference. It does not mean that every row below 60% is automatically an anomaly. Detector gates are still applied separately.

## 8.2 Gap severity

\[
gap = expected-actual
\]

It uses the same expected reference as the ratio.

\[
S_{gap} = clip\left(\frac{gap}{80\ Mbps},0,1\right)
\]

Examples:

- gap = 0 Mbps → 0;
- gap = 40 Mbps → 0.50;
- gap = 80 Mbps or more → 1.

The denominator spreads severity across a practical 0–80 Mbps deficit range.

## 8.3 Absolute low-throughput severity

\[
S_{low}=clip\left(\frac{20-actual}{20},0,1\right)
\]

This component does not compare against P10, IQR, MAD, or ML.

It compares actual throughput with a fixed 20 Mbps **severity reference**.

Examples:

- actual = 20 Mbps → 0;
- actual = 10 Mbps → 0.50;
- actual = 0 Mbps → 1.

This is not a separate 20 Mbps detector. It prevents the score from treating 2 Mbps and 18 Mbps as equally severe merely because both triggered another method.

## 8.4 Temporal-drop severity

The notebook calculates:

- rolling-median gap;
- previous-sample drop.

It uses the larger of the two:

\[
drop = \max(rolling\ median-actual,\ previous\ sample-actual)
\]

Then:

\[
S_{drop}=clip\left(\frac{drop}{40\ Mbps},0,1\right)
\]

The notebook now exports whether the dominant drop source was:

- `rolling_median_gap`; or
- `previous_sample_gap`.

## Relationship to the main anomaly families

- Ratio and gap mainly describe **expected-underperformance** cases.
- Drop mainly describes **temporal-change** cases.
- Low describes absolute degradation and supports all cases.
- RB-efficiency has a separate severity term that can raise the magnitude floor.

The score dimensions measure severity. They do not replace the detector families.

# 10. Why were 0.80 and 0.90 used in fused confidence?

The previous simplified confidence formula was:

\[
C = 1-(1-0.80E_{stat})(1-0.90E_{ML})+bonuses
\]

## What those values meant

They were not probabilities and were not the final 55%/45% priority weights.

They acted as evidence ceilings:

- even perfect statistical evidence could contribute at most 0.80 before bonuses;
- even perfect ML evidence could contribute at most 0.90 before bonuses.

The noisy-OR structure allowed two independent sources to strengthen one another without directly adding to more than 1.

For example, if:

\[
E_{stat}=0.7,\quad E_{ML}=0.6
\]

then:

\[
C_{base}=1-(1-0.80\cdot0.7)(1-0.90\cdot0.6)
\]

\[
=1-(1-0.56)(1-0.54)
\]

\[
=1-(0.44)(0.46)=0.7976
\]

before bonuses.

## Why the ordering was questionable

The project explicitly defines statistical methods as the primary anomaly detectors. A larger ML ceiling than statistical ceiling made that design harder to defend.

## Updated named ceilings

The review-adjusted notebook uses:

```text
FUSION_STAT_EVIDENCE_CEILING = 0.90
FUSION_ML_EVIDENCE_CEILING   = 0.85
```

The base confidence becomes:

\[
C_{base}=1-(1-0.90E_{stat})(1-0.85E_{ML})
\]

This better reflects the statistical-first architecture while allowing strong ML evidence to contribute substantially.

## Updated bounded bonuses

The notebook adds small explicit bonuses:

- 0.07 for statistical–ML agreement;
- 0.03 for confirmed medium/high RB demand;
- 0.03 when both production ML families support the row.

The result is clipped to [0,1].

## Exported confidence components

The notebook now preserves:

- statistical component;
- ML component;
- noisy-OR base;
- agreement bonus;
- RB-demand bonus;
- two-ML-family bonus.

This makes the final confidence auditable row by row.

# 11. Detailed final row-level fusion

The final row-level fusion separates **impact** from **confidence**.

## 11.1 Statistical evidence

Statistical strength is derived from the uncapped statistical anomaly score:

\[
strength_{stat}=clip(score_{stat}/100,0,1)
\]

It is multiplied by a reliability factor based on the type of statistical evidence.

The main reliability ordering is:

- reliable expected-underperformance, RB-efficiency, or CA evidence: highest;
- radio-limited evidence: high;
- temporal evidence: high;
- sustained-only evidence: moderate;
- generic low-tail-only evidence: lower.

Thus, an IQR-only low-throughput row does not receive the same confidence as a row with reliable P75 underperformance and high RB demand.

## 11.2 ML evidence

Only two independent production families influence final confidence:

### Resource family

Built from the strongest evidence from:

- resource Q75;
- HGB resource-conditioned central prediction.

These are correlated and therefore count as one family.

### Temporal family

Built from:

- HGB strict-causal temporal prediction.

LSTM is excluded from production confidence.

Isolation Forest is excluded from production confidence.

ML family confidence uses a noisy-OR combination:

\[
E_{ML}=1-(1-E_{resource})(1-E_{temporal})
\]

## 11.3 Impact families

The notebook calculates separate impact for:

- capability/radio-condition expected throughput;
- resource-conditioned expected throughput;
- temporal expected throughput;
- radio-limited degradation.

It chooses the strongest technically active family as the dominant impact family.

This prevents unrelated expected values from being combined into one unrealistic maximum.

### Expected-throughput impact

For an expected value \(e\) and actual value \(a\):

\[
gap=e-a
\]

\[
ratio=a/e
\]

The family impact combines:

- absolute low-throughput severity;
- expected-minus-actual gap severity;
- actual/expected ratio severity.

Temporal impact additionally includes the local-drop component.

### Radio-limited impact

Radio-limited impact avoids using an inflated capability maximum. It is based mainly on:

- absolute throughput degradation;
- persistence/bad-window evidence.

This is important because poor radio may correctly explain the low throughput, but it still represents a real coverage/planning problem.

## 11.4 Final row priority

\[
Priority_{row}=0.55\times Impact+0.45\times Confidence
\]

Impact receives slightly more weight because RCA priority should primarily reflect how damaging the event was. Confidence determines how strongly the evidence supports that interpretation.

## 11.5 Final keep paths

A row may be retained through:

1. statistical and ML consensus;
2. statistical expected/resource evidence;
3. statistical radio-limited evidence;
4. statistical temporal evidence;
5. balanced supervised ML-only evidence.

# 12. Out-of-fold prediction explained in detail

## 12.1 The leakage problem

Drive-test samples one second apart are highly correlated.

A random row split can place:

- sample at 00:10:20 in training;
- sample at 00:10:21 in testing.

The model effectively sees almost the same context during training and testing. The resulting R² can be unrealistically optimistic.

## 12.2 Group-based OOF design

The notebook uses session ID as the grouping variable.

With five folds, the sessions are divided into five mutually exclusive groups.

For fold 1:

- train on session groups 2–5;
- predict group 1.

For fold 2:

- train on groups 1, 3, 4, 5;
- predict group 2.

This continues until every row has a prediction from a model that never trained on that row’s session.

These predictions are called **out-of-fold predictions**.

## 12.3 Simple example

Assume five sessions:

```text
S1, S2, S3, S4, S5
```

A five-fold plan can behave as:

```text
Fold 1 test: S1 | train: S2 S3 S4 S5
Fold 2 test: S2 | train: S1 S3 S4 S5
Fold 3 test: S3 | train: S1 S2 S4 S5
Fold 4 test: S4 | train: S1 S2 S3 S5
Fold 5 test: S5 | train: S1 S2 S3 S4
```

After all folds, every session has predictions, but no session was predicted by a model trained on itself.

## 12.4 Why OOF is used for anomaly scoring

Historical anomaly labels must be based on predictions that behave like unseen-data predictions.

Using a final model fitted on all rows to score the same rows would underestimate residuals because the model has already learned from those targets.

Therefore:

- OOF predictions are used for historical anomaly decisions;
- final all-data models are saved only for future inference.

## 12.5 Nested robust-reference filtering

The expected-throughput model should learn normal or representative behavior, not severe anomalies.

The notebook creates a robust reference mask inside each outer training fold.

The critical rule is:

> The held-out session must not influence which training rows are removed.

This is why filtering is nested inside the fold rather than calculated once on the complete dataset.

## 12.6 Q75 calibration inside training

The Q75 correction is also derived from training-only inner OOF predictions.

The outer held-out sessions do not influence:

- the base Q75 model;
- the correction;
- the robust reference mask.

This avoids calibration leakage.

## 12.7 OOF metrics versus final artifact

Two different model objects exist conceptually:

### Historical validation models

Used fold by fold to produce OOF predictions and honest metrics.

### Final future-inference artifact

Refitted after validation using the accepted reference rows. This model is saved for future unseen drive-test data.

The final artifact should never be used to claim historical held-out performance.

# 13. LSTM explained in detail

## 13.1 What the LSTM predicts

The LSTM uses a causal window of the previous 20 seconds to predict the current throughput.

For target time \(t\), the input is:

\[
[t-20,t-19,\ldots,t-1]
\]

The target is throughput at:

\[
t
\]

The sequence never includes \(t+1\) or any future sample.

## 13.2 Example

Suppose the throughput values are:

```text
Time:       t-5  t-4  t-3  t-2  t-1   t
Throughput:  80   78   81   79   77   15 Mbps
```

The LSTM sees the previous sequence and may predict a central expected value near 78 Mbps.

Actual throughput is 15 Mbps.

Then:

```text
Expected gap = 78 - 15 = 63 Mbps
Actual/expected ratio = 15 / 78 = 0.192
```

This provides strong temporal underperformance evidence.

## 13.3 Input features

Each second in the sequence contains standardized causal features, plus standardized log past throughput.

The sequence may include:

- radio KPIs;
- resource KPIs;
- link-adaptation KPIs;
- CA context;
- past-only event indicators;
- past throughput.

Symmetric ±window event features are not allowed.

## 13.4 Architecture

The implemented architecture is:

1. one LSTM layer;
2. hidden size 48;
3. last hidden output;
4. linear layer reducing hidden size to half;
5. ReLU;
6. dropout;
7. final linear scalar output.

The prediction is made in log-throughput space.

## 13.5 Why log throughput is used

Throughput is positive and can be strongly skewed.

The transformation:

\[
y_{log}=\log(1+y)
\]

reduces the influence of extreme high-throughput samples and makes multiplicative errors easier to learn.

The output is converted back using:

\[
\hat y=\exp(\hat y_{log})-1
\]

## 13.6 Q50 pinball loss

The LSTM uses quantile loss with \(q=0.50\):

\[
L_q(y,\hat y)=\max(q(y-\hat y),(q-1)(y-\hat y))
\]

For \(q=0.50\), this is proportional to absolute error and targets the conditional median.

## 13.7 Training controls

The notebook uses:

- AdamW optimizer;
- learning rate 0.001;
- weight decay;
- gradient clipping;
- session-level validation split;
- early stopping;
- maximum 35 epochs;
- patience of six epochs.

## 13.8 Sequence coverage limitation

The first 20 samples of every session cannot receive an LSTM prediction because no full 20-second history exists.

Short sessions may produce no LSTM sequences.

This is one reason the LSTM is retained as validation context rather than required for final anomaly coverage.

## 13.9 Why LSTM remains context-only

The LSTM is useful for checking whether a sequence model agrees with the simpler temporal HGB model.

However, it introduces:

- greater computational cost;
- lower coverage;
- more hyperparameters;
- more difficult deployment;
- potentially less stable generalization.

It therefore does not contribute to final production confidence or compact RCA JSON.

# 15. Radio-limited anomalies and planning relevance

Radio-limited degradation remains a valid and important anomaly case.

It means:

1. throughput is genuinely degraded;
2. the degradation is accompanied by poor radio evidence;
3. the likely RCA domain is coverage, interference, dominance, mobility, or planning rather than unexplained scheduler/application behavior.

## Why it must not be removed

A throughput detector should not exclude a case merely because bad RSRP, SINR, RSRQ, or BLER can explain the throughput.

The job of anomaly detection is to identify abnormal service impact. The job of RCA is to explain it.

A radio-limited incident may indicate:

- coverage hole;
- overshooting cell;
- weak dominance;
- pilot pollution;
- interference;
- antenna tilt or azimuth issue;
- missing neighbor;
- mobility failure;
- poor serving-cell selection;
- border/edge planning issue.

## Updated handling

Radio-limited rows now receive:

```text
coverage_planning_review_flag = true
```

The final JSON contains a `planning_relevance` object.

The radio-only keep path was also broadened so a radio-limited row can be retained when it is supported by:

- persistence;
- very low throughput;
- temporal drop evidence;
- reliable expected-underperformance evidence.

# 16. Why MAD uses 0.6745 and a threshold of -3.0

## 16.1 MAD definition

For transformed throughput values \(x_i\):

\[
median = med(x)
\]

\[
MAD = med(|x_i-median|)
\]

The modified z-score is:

\[
M_i=0.6745\frac{x_i-median}{MAD}
\]

The notebook applies this to `log1p(throughput)`.

## 16.2 Why 0.6745?

For a standard normal distribution:

\[
median(|X|) \approx 0.67448975
\]

This is also the 75th percentile of a standard normal variable.

Multiplying by approximately 0.6745 makes the MAD-based score comparable to the usual standard-deviation z-score under normally distributed data.

Without this constant, the raw MAD scale would not align with standard z-score interpretation.

## 16.3 It is not -3 dB

The threshold is **-3.0 modified-z units**, not -3 dB.

It is dimensionless.

It does not represent:

- RSRP change;
- RSRQ change;
- SINR change;
- a 3 dB power ratio.

## 16.4 Why -3.0 rather than -3.5?

A common robust-z rule uses approximately ±3.5.

In this project, the detector is applied after `log1p()` compression. The earlier -3.5 lower threshold was too strict for this dataset and could produce no useful extreme lower-tail rows.

The -3.0 threshold was selected to preserve an extreme, data-adaptive lower-tail detector while retaining enough cases for validation.

It remains subject to the exported threshold-calibration and overlap reports. It should not be treated as a universal telecom constant.

longitude_{approx}=median(longitude_i)
\]

The median is used instead of the mean because it is less sensitive to GPS outliers.

## Observation-spread estimate

The notebook calculates the distance of each anomaly GPS point from the median coordinate and reports the 90th-percentile distance.

This gives:

```text
p90_observation_spread_m
```

A small value means anomaly samples were spatially concentrated.

A large value means the ECI was observed across a wider route area, so the marker is less precise.

## Critical limitation

The coordinate is not guaranteed to be the physical tower coordinate.

It is:

> the median drive-test location where the device observed the anomaly while served by that ECI/cell.

A physical site location requires a planning database containing cell/site coordinates.

The exported GeoJSON is still useful for the dashboard because it places the service-impact region on the route.

## Cell identity

ECI is preferred. When ECI is unavailable, the notebook uses:

```text
EARFCN + PCI
```

PCI alone is not sufficient because PCI can be reused in different geographic areas.

# 18. Episode creation in detail

## 18.1 Why row-level anomalies are not enough

With one sample per second, a 20-second network problem can create 20 anomaly rows.

Sending all rows independently to RCA would:

- inflate the anomaly count;
- make one event appear as many separate faults;
- create repetitive JSON records;
- make prioritization difficult.

Episodes represent continuous periods of degraded behavior.

## 18.2 Row-to-episode grouping

Rows are first sorted by:

- session;
- timestamp;
- source order.

A row remains in the same episode when:

- it belongs to the same session;
- it remains an anomaly row;
- the time gap is within the configured episode continuity limit.

The current row-level episode gap allows up to three seconds. This tolerates a brief missing or unflagged sample without fragmenting one clear degradation period.

## 18.3 Episode duration

For start time \(t_s\) and end time \(t_e\):

\[
duration=t_e-t_s
\]

Because sampling is one second, inclusive span is:

\[
span=duration+1\ second
\]

A one-row episode therefore has:

- duration = 0 seconds;
- inclusive observed span = 1 second.

## 18.4 Observed anomalous seconds

\[
observed\ anomalous\ seconds = rows \times 1\ second
\]

## 18.5 Episode density

\[
density=\frac{observed\ anomalous\ seconds}{inclusive\ span}
\]

Examples:

- 10 anomaly rows in a 10-second inclusive span → density = 1.0;
- 5 anomaly rows in a 10-second inclusive span → density = 0.5.

Density distinguishes continuously bad episodes from sparse detections bridged across small gaps.

## 18.6 Longest consecutive run

The notebook also calculates the longest sequence where adjacent anomaly rows remain approximately one second apart.

This prevents a long but sparse episode from receiving the same persistence interpretation as a fully consecutive degradation.

---

# 19. Episode persistence in detail

The updated persistence score is:

\[
Persistence=100\left(
0.40S_{anomaly\ seconds}
+0.30S_{span}
+0.20S_{longest\ run}
+0.10S_{density}
\right)
\]

where:

\[
S_{anomaly\ seconds}=clip\left(\frac{observed\ anomalous\ seconds}{10},0,1\right)
\]

\[
S_{span}=clip\left(\frac{inclusive\ span}{30},0,1\right)
\]

\[
S_{longest\ run}=clip\left(\frac{longest\ consecutive\ run}{10},0,1\right)
\]

and density is already between zero and one.

## Interpretation

- a one-second isolated anomaly has low persistence;
- a ten-second consecutive anomaly receives strong persistence;
- a 30-second sustained episode approaches maximum span contribution;
- sparse detections receive a lower density contribution.

## Example

Assume:

- observed anomaly seconds = 8;
- inclusive span = 10 seconds;
- longest consecutive run = 6 seconds;
- density = 0.8.

Then:

\[
S_{anomaly\ seconds}=0.8
\]

\[
S_{span}=10/30=0.333
\]

\[
S_{run}=0.6
\]

\[
Persistence=100(0.40\cdot0.8+0.30\cdot0.333+0.20\cdot0.6+0.10\cdot0.8)
\]

\[
\approx 62.0
\]

# 20. Episode impact in detail

The updated episode impact is:

\[
Impact_{episode}=
0.50\times MaxRowImpact
+0.25\times MeanRowImpact
+0.25\times Persistence
\]

All components are normalized to [0,1] before conversion to 0–100.

## Why maximum and mean are both used

### Maximum row impact

Captures the worst second.

A severe one-second collapse should not disappear inside a longer moderate episode.

### Mean row impact

Captures the typical level across the episode.

This prevents one extreme point from making an otherwise mild interval appear fully critical.

### Persistence

Represents how long and continuously the degradation lasted.

## Example

Assume:

- max row impact = 90;
- mean row impact = 60;
- persistence = 62.

\[
Impact_{episode}=0.50(0.90)+0.25(0.60)+0.25(0.62)
\]

\[
=0.755
\]

Episode impact = 75.5.

---

# 21. Episode confidence in detail

The updated episode confidence is:

\[
Confidence_{episode}=
0.50\times MaxRowConfidence
+0.25\times MeanRowConfidence
+0.15\times ConsensusCoverage
+0.10\times DirectEvidenceCoverage
\]

## Maximum row confidence

Represents the strongest-supported point in the episode.

## Mean row confidence

Represents stability of evidence across the episode.

## Consensus coverage

\[
ConsensusCoverage=\frac{statistical\ and\ ML\ consensus\ rows}{episode\ rows}
\]

A high value means multiple independent detector classes agreed during much of the episode.

## Direct evidence coverage

The notebook counts rows supported by direct evidence such as:

- P75 underperformance;
- RB-conditioned underperformance;
- RB-efficiency failure;
- CA underperformance;
- radio-limited degradation;
- temporal drop;
- sustained degradation;
- production ML underperformance.

Then:

\[
DirectEvidenceCoverage=clip\left(\frac{direct\ evidence\ rows}{episode\ rows},0,1\right)
\]

This rewards episodes whose interpretation is supported throughout the interval.

## Example

Assume:

- max row confidence = 85;
- mean row confidence = 70;
- consensus coverage = 0.50;
- direct evidence coverage = 0.80.

\[
Confidence_{episode}=0.50(0.85)+0.25(0.70)+0.15(0.50)+0.10(0.80)
\]

\[
=0.755
\]

Episode confidence = 75.5.

# 22. Episode final priority

Episode priority uses the same impact-confidence philosophy as row-level fusion:

\[
Priority_{episode}=0.55\times Impact_{episode}+0.45\times Confidence_{episode}
\]

For the example where both are 75.5:

\[
Priority_{episode}=75.5
\]

An episode must also satisfy the final episode keep rules. A low-priority singleton statistical row requires stronger evidence than a multi-row persistent episode.

# 23. Operational incident consolidation

## 23.1 Why operational incidents are used

One real network event can be split into several episodes because of:

- a one- or two-second throughput recovery;
- a temporarily missing KPI;
- a detector threshold crossing;
- a small GPS discontinuity;
- a model prediction becoming unavailable for one sample.

Without consolidation, RCA may receive three incidents that are actually one event.

Operational incidents bridge only very close, technically compatible strict episodes.

## 23.2 Bridging conditions

Two consecutive strict episodes can be merged when all relevant checks pass.

### Same session

Episodes must belong to the same drive-test session.

### Small time gap

\[
0 \leq gap \leq 5\ seconds
\]

### Small spatial gap

The distance from the previous episode end to the next episode start must be:

\[
distance \leq 50\ meters
\]

If GPS is unavailable, the spatial check is treated as unknown rather than automatically failing.

### Compatible serving cell

The notebook prefers the complete serving-cell key.

If both episodes have a cell key, they must match.

If the cell key is unavailable, PCI compatibility is used as a fallback.

### Compatible anomaly family

At least one of the following must agree:

- dominant statistical type;
- dominant ML type;
- dominant impact family.

This prevents unrelated problems occurring close in time from being merged merely because the device remained nearby.

## 23.3 Example

Episode A:

- 03:16:03 to 03:16:08;
- ECI 1234;
- radio-limited;
- ends at route point X.

Episode B:

- starts at 03:16:11;
- ECI 1234;
- radio-limited;
- 18 meters from X.

Checks:

- time gap = 3 seconds → pass;
- distance = 18 meters → pass;
- same ECI → pass;
- same radio impact family → pass.

They become one operational incident.

If Episode B were on a different ECI and represented CA underperformance, it would remain separate.

## 23.4 Bridge audit

The notebook exports:

```text
episode_to_operational_incident_bridge_audit.csv
```

For each episode, it records whether it:

- started a new incident; or
- was bridged to the previous episode.

The reason string contains the evaluated gap, distance, cell compatibility, and family compatibility.

# 26. Final interpretation guidance

The detector output should be interpreted in three layers.

## Detection

What evidence says the throughput behavior is abnormal?

Examples:

- P10/IQR/MAD;
- temporal drop;
- P75 underperformance;
- RB efficiency;
- causal ML residual.

## Case classification

What broad technical condition accompanies it?

Examples:

- radio-limited;
- CA underperformance;
- resource-conditioned underperformance;
- temporal collapse;
- ML-only supervised review.

## RCA

What network cause explains the condition?

Examples:

- weak coverage;
- interference;
- missing neighbor;
- scheduler limitation;
- poor MCS caused by bad CQI;
- high BLER and retransmissions;
- CA activation without useful secondary-cell contribution;
- application/server limitation;
- low offered traffic.

The anomaly detector does not claim the final root cause. It produces a prioritized, compact, evidence-rich incident that allows the RCA team to investigate efficiently.
